The Art of Accessorizing: Styling Notes From Our Team

Of all the styling questions our team fields in the boutique, accessorizing comes up more than almost anything else. Clients will often have a strong sense of what clothing they like, but describe accessories as the part of getting dressed that feels the most arbitrary — something added at the last minute, or skipped entirely because there wasn't an obvious answer for what belonged.

In practice, accessorizing is one of the more teachable parts of styling, because it follows a handful of consistent principles rather than depending purely on instinct. Here's how our stylists actually approach it when they're helping someone finish a look.

It's also worth saying that accessorizing well doesn't require a large collection. Some of the most consistently well-put-together clients we work with own a genuinely small number of accessories, chosen carefully and understood well, rather than a large, loosely curated collection where most pieces rarely get worn. The principles below work the same way regardless of how big or small that collection is.

Start with the outfit's existing focal point

Every outfit already has something drawing the eye, whether that's a bold color, an interesting cut, or a distinctive fabric. The first accessorizing decision is figuring out what that focal point already is, so you don't accidentally compete with it. If a piece is already doing a lot of visual work, most accessories should support it quietly rather than add a second, competing statement.

This is the most common accessorizing mistake we see: a genuinely interesting outfit gets buried under an equally loud bag, a busy print scarf, and statement jewelry all fighting for attention at once. Pick one thing to be the star of the outfit, and let the accessories either reinforce it or stay quiet.

Use metal tones as a simple organizing principle

One of the fastest ways to make an accessory selection feel intentional rather than random is to stay consistent with metal tones across jewelry, hardware, and any metal detailing on shoes or bags. It doesn't need to be a strict rule for every single occasion, but defaulting to one metal family per outfit removes a surprising amount of decision fatigue and instantly reads as more considered.

If you genuinely love mixing metals, that's a legitimate style choice too — it just tends to work best as a deliberate statement rather than an accident of whatever was already in your jewelry box that morning.

Let scale do more work than color

When people describe an accessory as feeling "off," it's often a scale problem rather than a color problem. A delicate necklace can disappear against a heavy, textured knit, while the same necklace looks considered against a simple, fine cotton shirt. Before reaching for a different color or style, try adjusting the scale of what you're already holding — a slightly larger earring, a chunkier bag strap, a wider belt — and see if that solves the problem before you assume the piece itself is wrong.

Bags and shoes don't have to match, but they should agree

The old rule that bags and shoes must match in color is mostly outdated, but there's a more useful version of it worth keeping: they should agree in formality and general texture, even if the colors differ. A structured leather bag paired with worn canvas sneakers can create an unintentional tension, while a slightly more casual bag alongside the same sneakers reads as consistent. Think in terms of a formality "temperature" across your accessories rather than a strict color-matching rule.

One accessory can carry an entire outfit's personality

On days when getting dressed feels like too much decision-making, our stylists often suggest picking one accessory first — a specific bag, a favorite scarf, a distinctive pair of shoes — and building the rest of the outfit around keeping that one piece as the clear focus. This flips the usual order of getting dressed and tends to produce more interesting, less generic outfits than starting with the clothing and treating accessories as an afterthought.

Know your genuine "reach for it" pieces

Every accessory collection eventually reveals a small handful of pieces that get worn constantly, while the rest sit untouched in a drawer. Rather than treating that as a failure of the untouched pieces, it's worth paying attention to what your true favorites have in common — scale, color family, level of detail — and letting that shape future purchases, instead of continuing to buy accessories that don't match a pattern you've already proven you actually wear.

Building confidence with color through accessories first

For clients hesitant to bring bold color into their clothing, accessories are often the lowest-risk place to start experimenting. A small dose of color in a bag, a scarf, or a pair of shoes lets you gauge how a shade actually feels against your existing wardrobe and your own coloring, without committing to an entire garment in that color. If it works, it's easy to gradually bring more of that color into larger pieces later. If it doesn't feel right, replacing an accessory is a far smaller decision than replacing a full-price coat or dress.

This is one of the more practical, low-stakes ways to develop a stronger sense of your own color preferences over time, and it's a method our stylists suggest often to clients who describe themselves as generally cautious dressers looking to slowly expand their range.

Accessorizing for different seasons, not just different outfits

Accessory choices often get planned outfit by outfit, but they also shift meaningfully by season in ways worth planning for deliberately. Lighter, smaller-scale jewelry and bags tend to suit the simpler silhouettes of warmer months, while heavier layers in colder months can actually support slightly bolder or larger accessories without feeling overwhelming, since there's more fabric and structure in the outfit to balance against. Treating your accessory choices as something that rotates with the season, the same way your clothing does, tends to produce more consistently well-balanced outfits than reaching for the same handful of pieces year-round regardless of what they're paired with.

When an accessory should be the investment piece, not the outfit

There's a reasonable case for spending more, proportionally, on a small number of accessories than on the clothing they pair with, since a well-made bag, belt, or pair of shoes often gets worn across dozens of different outfits over several years, spreading its cost and its wear far more widely than any single piece of clothing typically does. A quality leather bag paired with simple, less expensive basics frequently reads as more put-together than an expensive outfit paired with a bag that's already showing wear.

This is part of why our stylists often encourage clients to think of a handful of accessories as genuine long-term investments, worth researching and choosing carefully, even while keeping the rest of the wardrobe more flexible and budget-conscious.

A few working rules from our stylists

  • Identify the outfit's focal point before adding anything else.
  • Default to one metal family per outfit unless mixing is a deliberate choice.
  • If an accessory feels "off," try adjusting scale before changing color.
  • Match formality and texture between bags and shoes, not necessarily color.
  • On low-effort days, pick one accessory first and build outward from it.
  • Let a small number of accessories be the wardrobe's real investment pieces.

Accessorizing well isn't about owning more pieces. It's about understanding a small set of relationships — focal point, scale, metal tone, formality — well enough to make quick, confident decisions with whatever you already own. That's a skill that improves with attention, not with a bigger collection.
